Introduction¶

For this project, I wanted to better understand how employment and wages have shifted across different industries in the U.S. over time. I focused on data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) because it provides detailed monthly statistics on employment, unemployment, and earnings by industry. The data is extensive and captures historical trends from 2006 to 2023, which allows me to explore how major economic events like the 2008 recession and the COVID-19 pandemic impacted different sectors.

What drew me to this dataset is that employment trends often reflect the broader health of the economy. I was curious to see which industries showed growth, which struggled, and how workers' earnings evolved across different time periods. My goal was to track not just how many people were working, but also whether their pay changed — and how that varied by industry.

The three main fields I focused on were:

  • Industry sector — to see which areas of the economy were driving job creation.
  • Employment numbers — to understand how job counts changed over time.
  • Average weekly earnings — to evaluate whether workers’ wages were rising or stagnating.

My hypothesis was that healthcare and tech-related industries would show stronger resilience and consistent growth, while sectors like retail or manufacturing might be more sensitive to economic disruptions. I also wanted to understand whether some industries recovered faster than others after major downturns.

The population represented by this data includes nonfarm wage and salary workers across the U.S., with data collected from over 144,000 businesses and government agencies, representing around 697,000 worksites. While it’s not a full census, the CES program uses stratified sampling and weighting to ensure it captures national labor market trends accurately.

For this analysis, I used the full dataset across industry, wage, and employment dimensions. After merging and cleaning the data into a readable format, I explored trends from 2006 through 2023 to gain insight into how different parts of the job market evolved and what that says about the broader economy.

Data Explained¶

Data Source¶

The data comes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics CES. It includes employment and earnings data across various industries from 2006 to 2023.

Data Preparation¶

To get the dataset ready for analysis, I did the following:

  • Merged multiple CES data files using industry and data type codes.
  • Dropped unnecessary columns with mostly missing values.
  • Checked and removed duplicate rows (none were found).
  • Identified and removed outliers using the IQR method.
  • Converted the value column to numeric format.
  • Made sure there were no missing values in important columns.
  • Created a proper date column from year and month for time-based analysis.
  • Removed industries with missing records
  • Added a 'Sector' column to group industries for comparative analysis

Data Dictionary¶

Correlation Between Employment and Wage Growth¶

Analyzing the relationship between employment growth and wage growth can reveal whether industries are investing in workforce expansion, compensation, or both.

In [3]:
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# Make sure Year is integer
df['Year'] = df['Year'].astype(int)

# Step 1: Compute growth
emp = df[(df['Metric'] == 'ALL EMPLOYEES, THOUSANDS') & df['Year'].isin([2006, 2023])]
emp_pivot = emp.pivot(index='Industry', columns='Year', values='Value').reset_index()
emp_pivot['Growth_Employment'] = emp_pivot[2023] - emp_pivot[2006]

wage = df[(df['Metric'] == 'AVERAGE WEEKLY EARNINGS OF ALL EMPLOYEES') & df['Year'].isin([2006, 2023])]
wage_pivot = wage.pivot(index='Industry', columns='Year', values='Value').reset_index()
wage_pivot['Growth_Wage'] = wage_pivot[2023] - wage_pivot[2006]

# Merge and classify
merged = pd.merge(
    emp_pivot[['Industry', 'Growth_Employment']],
    wage_pivot[['Industry', 'Growth_Wage']],
    on='Industry'
)

# Sector classification
def classify_sector(industry):
    industry = industry.lower()
    if any(k in industry for k in ['hospital', 'health', 'ambulatory', 'practitioner']):
        return 'Healthcare'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['software', 'computer', 'web search', 'technology']):
        return 'Technology'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['retail', 'retailer']):
        return 'Retail'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['manufactur', 'machinery', 'equipment']):
        return 'Manufacturing'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['finance', 'insurance', 'bank']):
        return 'Finance'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['education', 'school']):
        return 'Education'
    else:
        return 'Other'

merged['Sector'] = merged['Industry'].apply(classify_sector)
merged = merged.dropna()

# Step 2: Group by Sector and average
sector_summary = merged.groupby('Sector')[['Growth_Employment', 'Growth_Wage']].mean().sort_values(by='Growth_Employment', ascending=False)

# Step 3: Plot
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
sns.heatmap(sector_summary, annot=True, fmt=".1f", cmap='YlGnBu', linewidths=.5)
plt.title("Average Employment and Wage Growth by Sector (2006–2023)")
plt.xlabel("Metric")
plt.ylabel("Sector")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

These tables display the industries with the highest employment and wage growth over the analysis period.

Insights:¶

  • Healthcare stands out with strong growth in both employment and wages**, suggesting sustained demand, investment, and sector resilience — especially following economic disruptions like COVID-19.

  • Technology shows exceptionally high wage growth** alongside moderate employment growth, indicating increased demand for specialized digital skills and possibly improved worker productivity.

  • Manufacturing demonstrates modest wage increases but slightly negative employment growth, which may reflect the impact of automation, outsourcing, or structural shifts within the industry.

  • Retail is experiencing declines in employment and relatively low wage growth, likely due to the ongoing transition to e-commerce and reduced need for traditional retail jobs.

  • Other industries have the highest average employment growth, though this category includes a wide range of sectors — such as scientific and technical services — which may skew the results.

This visualization highlights how sector resilience and transformation patterns have varied significantly across the U.S. labor market between 2006 and 2023.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# Define economic events
economic_events = {
    '2008 Recession': (2007, 2009),
    'COVID-19 Pandemic': (2020, 2021)
}

# Define sector classification to evaluate the industries in a better way.
def classify_sector(industry):
    industry = industry.lower()
    if any(k in industry for k in ['hospital', 'health', 'ambulatory', 'practitioner']):
        return 'Healthcare'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['software', 'computer', 'web search', 'technology']):
        return 'Technology'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['retail', 'retailer']):
        return 'Retail'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['manufactur', 'machinery', 'equipment']):
        return 'Manufacturing'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['finance', 'insurance', 'bank']):
        return 'Finance'
    elif any(k in industry for k in ['education', 'school']):
        return 'Education'
    else:
        return 'Other'

# Apply classification
df['Sector'] = df['Industry'].apply(classify_sector)

# Filter and clean data
employment_data = df[df['Metric'] == 'ALL EMPLOYEES, THOUSANDS'].dropna(subset=['Value'])
wage_data = df[df['Metric'] == 'AVERAGE WEEKLY EARNINGS OF ALL EMPLOYEES'].dropna(subset=['Value'])

Insights:¶

Looking at the trends from 2006 to 2023, a few clear patterns stand out, especially when comparing how different sectors responded to major economic events like the 2008 recession and the COVID-19 pandemic.

During the 2008 recession, employment in manufacturing and retail dipped slightly or plateaued, but the overall impact wasn’t dramatic. Wages in these sectors held steady or even showed gradual increases. This suggests that while some job cuts may have happened, employers likely retained key roles and avoided slashing wages, possibly due to union contracts, wage stickiness, or simply needing to keep essential operations running.

In contrast, the COVID-19 pandemic brought a more immediate and sharper disruption. Employment in the "Other" sector saw a steep drop around 2020 and 2021, and wages in that group also briefly declined. That category may include service-heavy or nonessential industries that couldn’t easily pivot to remote work. Despite this, most sectors rebounded fairly quickly after 2021.

What’s striking is how consistently strong healthcare and technology were throughout both crises. These sectors not only avoided major employment drops, they kept growing. Wages also climbed steadily year over year. This points to their increasing importance in the economy, whether it’s due to the need for medical services or the rise of digital tools and remote infrastructure.

Technology in particular stands out for its wage growth. Even when job numbers didn’t surge dramatically, average pay kept rising. This likely reflects growing demand for specialized, high-skill roles like software engineers and IT professionals. Employers seem willing to pay a premium for these skills even during downturns.

Retail and manufacturing, on the other hand, were more up and down. Retail employment remained relatively flat overall, and wage increases were more modest. Manufacturing employment saw a longer recovery curve after 2008 and only modest wage growth in comparison to other sectors.

One thing that really stands out from both plots is that wages tend to move upward more smoothly than employment. Even when job numbers fluctuate, paychecks keep getting a little bigger. That could be due to labor shortages, inflation, or employers choosing to retain fewer but better compensated workers during uncertain times.

All in all, the side-by-side charts help paint a clear picture. Some sectors, especially healthcare and technology, are built to withstand shocks and keep moving forward. Others, like retail and manufacturing, feel the impact more quickly and take longer to recover. It’s a reminder that resilience in the labor market isn’t just about job counts. It’s also about how industries adapt, prioritize, and invest in their people.

Summary¶

The large dataset provided was massive and very hard to ensure we look at all the different perspectives. To get to here, I had to merge the files together in different ways to get what was needed to perform the analysis. In addition to this, a few industries had incomplete or inconsistent data, especially in recent years, which made it harder to capture a full picture across the board. Also, figuring out the best way to visualize everything wasn’t always straightforward. For instance, I initially tried bubble charts, but they weren’t the clearest — switching to heatmaps worked much better for communicating comparative growth.

After exploring employment and wage trends across industries from 2006 to 2023, a few clear patterns stood out. By looking at how different sectors behaved before, during, and after major events like the 2008 recession and the COVID-19 pandemic, we were able to uncover which industries stayed strong, which ones struggled, and how workers were affected along the way.

Here’s what stood out:

  • Healthcare and technology sectors showed reliable and consistent growth. Even during major economic disruptions, they continued to add jobs and increase wages, which speaks to their essential nature and long-term strength.

  • Retail and manufacturing were more up and down. Both sectors saw noticeable employment drops during downturns and tended to take longer to recover. Wage growth in these areas was also slower and more inconsistent.

  • Wages kept rising, even in some sectors where employment dropped — especially during the pandemic. This could mean employers were holding onto fewer but more specialized or higher-paid roles, or simply that tight labor markets pushed wages up.

  • Some industries added jobs without raising wages much, while others boosted pay without expanding their workforce. This mix of patterns suggests that different sectors are adapting in very different ways — some focusing on hiring, others on retention and pay.

Overall, this project helped me connect real-world labor data with broader economic shifts. It was interesting to see how different industries adapted (or didn’t) in response to major events like the recession and the pandemic, and how those choices showed up in both job counts and compensation.

Going forward, it would be great to build on this by bringing in more datasets from the same source. Adding in factors like inflation, cost of living, regional data, or demographics like age, gender, and part-time vs full-time status could help paint a more detailed picture of who was most affected and how. There’s also a lot of potential to explore forecasting models to see where things might be heading based on the patterns we’ve already seen.
